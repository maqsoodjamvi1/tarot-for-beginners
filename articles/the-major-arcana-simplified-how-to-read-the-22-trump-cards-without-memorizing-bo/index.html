When I first started learning tarot, staring at a 78-card deck felt like studying for a college final exam I hadn’t attended. The Minor Arcana made some intuitive sense—think of them like a standard deck of playing cards with suits, numbers, and everyday human drama. But the Major Arcana? Those 22 trump cards felt intimidating. They had grand, archaic names like The Hierophant, The Hanged Man, and The World, and guidebooks told me I needed to memorize rigid paragraphs of esoteric symbolism for every single one.

If you are standing at that same crossroads, trying to figure out if tarot is worth investing your time into, I have good news. You do not need a photographic memory to understand the Major Arcana. In fact, if you try to memorize a book definition for all 22 cards, you will likely freeze up the moment you actually try to do a reading for yourself.

Instead, it helps to look at these cards as a single, sequential story. Here is how to strip away the intimidating mystique and start reading the Major Arcana today using plain logic and a bit of imagination.

Meet the Fool’s Journey

The easiest way to understand the 22 cards of the Major Arcana without a cheat sheet is to view them as a cinematic script called “The Fool’s Journey.”

Imagine card number zero, The Fool, stepping off a cliff with a small bag and a loyal dog at his heels. He is naive, optimistic, and ready to experience life. Every single card that follows him in the deck is a life lesson, a person, an obstacle, or a milestone he encounters as he grows up, makes mistakes, and gains wisdom, ultimately ending at card 21, The World.

When you look at the Major Arcana this way, you stop seeing 22 disconnected flashcards. You see a narrative arc. The early cards (The Magician, The High Priestess, The Empress) represent foundational tools and influences. The middle cards (Strength, The Hermit, Wheel of Fortune) represent trials, internal growth, and sudden shifts. The later cards (Death, The Tower, The Sun) represent profound transformation and eventual mastery.

Use Visual Translation Instead of Memorization

Tarot creators packed these cards with visual clues long before guidebooks existed. When a Major Arcana card pops up in a spread, close the book and just look at the picture. Ask yourself a very simple question: What is actually happening in this drawing?

Take The Tower, for example. A lightning bolt strikes a stone fortress, crowns are flying off, and people are tumbling headfirst into the dark. You don’t need a dictionary to tell you what that means. It clearly depicts a sudden, shocking disruption that knocks down rigid structures.

Or consider The Hermit. An old man stands alone on a snowy mountain peak holding a glowing lantern in the dark. Again, the visual does the heavy lifting: he is taking a deliberate timeout from society to search his own soul for answers.

When you pull a card, try this three-step visual scan before checking your notes:

  • The Environment: Is it bright and sunny, stormy, barren, or lush?
  • The Body Language: Is the figure standing tall, hanging upside down, hiding behind swords, or opening their arms?
  • The Focal Point: Where does your eye go first? Is it a glowing star, a heavy door, a spinning wheel, or a drawn sword?

Group the Cards Into Practical Themes

If visual translation isn’t enough and you want a mental anchor, sort the 22 cards into three basic categories based on how they show up in real life. This drastically reduces the cognitive load of learning them:

  • The Internal Shift Cards: These deal with your mindset, intuition, and spiritual growth. Think of cards like The High Priestess (trust your gut), The Hermit (take a break to reflect), and The Moon (face your hidden anxieties).
  • The Action and Agency Cards: These cards show up when you need to make something happen, take control, or apply willpower. Think of The Magician (you have the tools you need), The Chariot (steer your life forward with discipline), and Justice (make a fair, balanced choice).
  • The External Force Cards: These represent moments where life happens to you, reminding you that you cannot control everything. Think of Wheel of Fortune (luck and timing), The Tower (inevitable upheaval), and Death (hard endings that make space for new beginnings).

When a Major Arcana card appears in a spread, it acts like a flashing neon sign. While the Minor Arcana cards talk about the daily weather of your life, the Major Arcana cards talk about the climate—the major life themes, turning points, and soul-level lessons you are currently navigating.

Learning tarot doesn’t require sealing yourself in a room with a massive leather-bound manual. By treating the Major Arcana as a chronological story and trusting your own eyes to translate the imagery, you can sit down with a deck today and pull a meaningful reading. As you start practicing with these foundational concepts, you’ll naturally discover which spreads and interpretations fit your personal intuition best.
